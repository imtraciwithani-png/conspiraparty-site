Heads, Tails and Hot Air

Posted 11/08/2026 · ← All field reports

Full disclosure: this is commentary and jokes. Facts come from the linked reports, so click through and judge for yourself. The party kits linked here are our own products.

It's Sunday, the day of rest, and the Agency's evidence locker is full of things that were supposed to settle arguments: a coin, an air-quality sensor, a cloud and an asteroid. Every one of them was treated like proof. Every one of them came with a twist. Pour the coffee. Open the folder.

XFacebookPinterest

Exhibit A: The sheriff's quarter

In Robertson County, Kentucky (the state's smallest county), the 2nd District magistrate race ended dead even: 91 votes for Republican Benny Ray Renner, 91 for Democrat Kristen Fryman. Out of 182 ballots, not one vote separated them, WLKY reports. Kentucky law calls for a drawing when candidates tie, so officials did the most American thing possible. According to WEKU, the county clerk flipped a quarter borrowed from the sheriff's pocket. Renner, listed first on the ballot, called heads. It was tails. Fryman went home with the seat and the quarter.

The Agency is braced for the hearings. Somewhere a pundit is already asking who minted that quarter, whether the sheriff has ties to Big Tails, and why nobody ran a forensic audit of George Washington's face. To be clear, there is zero evidence of anything except gravity. Both candidates reportedly laughed about it, which makes this the most gracious election outcome of the year. And it wasn't a fluke: WKDZ counted coin flips settling city council seats in six Kentucky towns. Next time someone says their vote doesn't matter, hand them a quarter.

Exhibit B: The sensor that smelled a rat (or something)

Up in Toronto, TorontoToday reports two tenants, who perform as the comedy duo Creepy Boys, say their live-in landlord is trying to evict them after an Airthings air monitor kept logging spikes in volatile organic compounds. The landlord's emails suggested smoking. The tenants say they don't smoke inside and that the culprit is, in their words on their GoFundMe, that they "fart too much." The landlord served a notice saying she wants the whole duplex for personal use, and the Landlord and Tenant Board's decision is still pending, so nobody has been ruled guilty of anything, gaseous or otherwise.

What the Agency loves is the investigation. The tenants told the paper they skipped the gas stove for 48 hours, went a day without hot water and a day without electricity, and the readings still spiked. They dropped when nobody was home. That is a cleaner controlled experiment than half the "studies" in your uncle's group chat. Skeptic lesson: a sensor tells you that something changed, not who did it or why. Data without context is just a number waiting for a conspiracy.

Exhibit C: A flying saucer with a weather permit

Tenerife got a visitor Saturday. A layered, saucer-shaped cloud hovered near Mount Teide as Storm Bruna rolled in, per Tenerife Weekly and Canarian Weekly. It's a lenticular cloud, formed when moist air rides over a mountain in waves. Locals even have a nickname for it: "el sombrero del Teide," Teide's hat. It can look parked in place while winds roar right through it. Somewhere a blurry phone photo of this is about to become "the Canary Islands incident," complete with a guy who swears it blinked. It didn't. It's a hat.

Exhibit D: The asteroid that won't follow the rules

For real science weirdness, ScienceAlert reports that the tiny asteroid 1998 KY26, about 36 feet across, keeps accelerating in ways it shouldn't. One theory was that it's secretly a "dark comet" venting invisible gas. New research in Icarus says it's too warm to keep the ice that would need. Sunlight pressure is still a suspect, and Japan's Hayabusa2 spacecraft arrives in 2031 to check. Notice how real scientists handle a mystery: they say "we don't know yet," test an idea, toss it when it fails and go look. No alien pilot required. (We'd still watch that press conference.)

The pattern on the corkboard

Today's theme is evidence that doesn't say what people want it to say. A coin doesn't pick sides. A sensor can't tell cigarette smoke from Sunday chili. A cloud isn't a craft. An odd asteroid isn't a cover-up. The skeptic move is the same every time: ask what else could explain it, then go find out. Preferably with snacks.
