Reptile Eyes and Silver Linings

Posted 11/22/2026 · ← All field reports

Full disclosure: this is commentary and jokes. Facts come from the linked reports, so click through and judge for yourself. The party kits linked here are our own products.

Sunday's file is about seeing what you came to see. A man blinked behind a mayor and the internet saw a lizard. An FBI database got hacked and somebody saw an upside. A rugby coach said nothing for 80 minutes and 900,000 people heard a playbook. Scientists went looking for a shipwreck and found a kitchen showroom. Grab the red string and squint.

XFacebookPinterest

Exhibit A: The reptilian eye-roll at City Hall

Per NDTV, a clip from New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani's November 20 speech shows a man standing behind him whose eyes roll up and, for a few frames, look white. Within hours the comments had a diagnosis: "reptilian glitch." IBTimes UK notes the clip itself is unverified, which has never once slowed down a comment section.

Our forensic lab has reviewed the footage and reached a conclusion. A man standing behind a politician during a speech rolled his eyes. That's not a lizard. That's a staffer. Anyone who has stood on a stage behind a podium for twenty minutes has done the exact same thing. Add livestream compression, bad lighting and a blink at the wrong frame, and you too can be a cold-blooded overlord. If the lizard people are real, their disguise department needs a performance review.

Exhibit B: The hack with a silver lining

According to Axios, President Trump told Fox News that a reported hack of the FBI's National Crime Information Center was "not good," then floated that it might help expose what he calls the "Russia, Russia, Russia hoax." The Independent quotes him: "maybe we'll learn things," and "I always like to put things in a positive spin."

Credit where due: that's the most honest description of politics we've heard all year. Spin isn't a scandal in Washington, it's the house sport, and every team plays. When the other side's files leak, it's accountability. When yours leak, it's a foreign attack on democracy. Somewhere a hacker is reading the coverage and wondering if they get a thank-you card or an indictment. Probably both. Bipartisan.

Exhibit C: The silent watchalong

South Africa rugby coach Rassie Erasmus is serving a six-match ban, so he can't contact the team on matchday. So he hosted an online watchalong where he barely said a word. Per IOL, it drew over 900,000 views and a pile of theories that he was secretly coaching through earbuds. RTÉ reports his assistant coaches denied communicating with him.

Think about that. A man sat quietly, sipped a drink and watched TV, and close to a million people decided it was a covert operation. That's the most-watched silent film since Charlie Chaplin. Our take: if you can coach a rugby team telepathically through a livestream, the ban isn't your problem. The CIA's recruiters are.

Exhibit D: The ocean's backrooms

ScienceAlert revisited a 2017 NOAA dive in the Gulf of Mexico. Scientists sent a robot down about 1.5 km to check out what sonar suggested might be a shipwreck. Instead they found a 40-foot shipping container and a field of lost fridges, freezers and washing machines, now home to corals and fish. NOAA's crew named it "Deep Clean."

So Atlantis isn't a lost civilization. It's a scratch-and-dent appliance outlet with no parking. Bonus exhibit from the same desk: per ScienceAlert, researchers named a fanged "vampire camel" from museum fossils collected decades ago. It roamed North America around 10 to 13 million years ago, and it ate plants. Fangs, a scary name and a salad habit: the most misleading branding since "Greenland."

The pattern on the corkboard

Today's theme is the Rorschach test. A blink becomes a lizard. A hack becomes a win. Silence becomes a game plan. A junk pile becomes a sunken city. Every one of these stories says more about the viewer than the thing being viewed. Ask the dumb questions, check the sources, and question everybody, especially us.
