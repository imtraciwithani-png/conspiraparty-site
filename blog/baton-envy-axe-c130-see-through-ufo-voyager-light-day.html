Why the Hell Shouldn’t I?

Posted 11/18/2026 · ← All field reports

Full disclosure: this is commentary and jokes. Facts come from the linked reports, so click through and judge for yourself. The party kits linked here are our own products.

Wednesday at the Agency has a theme, and a president said it out loud: "Why the hell shouldn't I get that?" Today's files are about wanting things you probably shouldn't have. A gold baton. A $60 million airplane, allegedly, with an axe. Proof of aliens from a cell phone photo. And one tiny spacecraft that only wants to be left alone. Grab the red string. Use it sparingly.

XFacebookPinterest

Exhibit A: Baton envy in Manila

At a state lunch at Malacañang Palace on Wednesday, President Trump recalled that Philippine President Manuel Quezon gave Gen. Douglas MacArthur a gold baton and the rank of field marshal in that very palace in 1936. Then he asked President Marcos for the same deal: "I'd like to have you do that to me, if you don't mind. I'd love that. I mean, why the hell shouldn't I get that?" Some guests laughed, and Marcos did not announce any appointment, per IBTimes UK. HuffPost filed it as "Trump Demands Another Honor."

Now the fair roast. Asking your lunch host for a field marshal rank, on camera, is a power move that makes regifting a fruitcake look shy. But one headline went with "Needy Trump, 80, Begs for Shiny Award," per The Daily Beast, which is less "news" and more "group chat with a masthead." IBTimes noted it was unclear whether he wanted the baton, the rank or both. The Agency suggests both, plus a tiny sash. If the theorists are right that every gift is a secret signal, the signal here is: please, somebody, just give the man a participation trophy and let lunch continue.

Exhibit B: The axe vs. the C-130

Federal prosecutors in New York have charged Daithi O'Corrain, 48, with destroying U.S. government property. They say that on April 11 he got onto the tarmac at Shannon Airport in Ireland, barricaded himself inside a parked Air National Guard C-130, cut wires and hydraulic lines, smashed gauges, then climbed out a roof hatch and hit the outside with an axe, per AFP and The Guardian. Prosecutors say the roughly $60 million plane "will not return to active flight status." These are allegations. He hasn't been tried, and no motive was given.

Here's what the Agency can roast without a lawyer: the security. According to the indictment, the crew found someone in their cockpit when they came back to leave. That's several hours inside a military plane at a major airport. Conspiracy culture loves a mastermind, but the most suspicious thing in this file is a padlock situation. Somewhere a TSA agent is still confiscating a grandma's shampoo.

Exhibit C: The see-through UFO of Wilsonville

An Oregon family told the National UFO Reporting Center that on Aug. 25 a huge silver, partly see-through object shone bright lights into their 12-year-old's bedroom window for about a minute, then vanished. Mom and daughter reported nausea and headaches that night and felt fine the next day. A NUFORC investigator checked local farm drones, police reports and FlightRadar24, found nothing, and labeled it "anomalous," per the NUFORC report. IBTimes UK points out that the size estimates are witness guesses and nothing links the symptoms to the object.

So the headlines went from "anomalous" to "UFO made them sick" in about three months. Meanwhile, a SETI scientist is out here telling Starlust that most UFO sightings can be explained. Both things can be true. The Agency's ruling: a see-through craft the width of three airplanes is either alien tech or the best argument yet for blackout curtains.

Exhibit D: One light-day of peace and quiet

At 10:16 UTC this morning (3:16 a.m. Mountain), Voyager 1 became the first spacecraft to reach one light-day from Earth, about 16.1 billion miles, per NASA. A command now takes 24 hours to get there and another 24 for the reply, Voyager scientist Linda Spilker told Space.com. It launched in 1977 and took 49 years to get this far.

Think about it. The one government project that's truly unreachable is the one everybody agrees is real. Nobody's calling Voyager a hoax. It's 49 years old, it answers every message in two days, and it doesn't post. Honestly, that's the healthiest relationship anyone has with the internet right now.

The pattern on the corkboard

Every file today has a gap between what happened and the headline. A lunch joke became a demand or a beg, depending on your feed. An accusation became a movie. A "we couldn't explain it" became a "they made us sick." The Agency's rule for Wednesday: read the source, then pick your outrage. Then laugh.
