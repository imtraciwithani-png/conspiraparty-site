Flat Tops and Round Objects

Posted 11/09/2026 · ← All field reports

Full disclosure: this is commentary and jokes. Facts come from the linked reports, so click through and judge for yourself. The party kits linked here are our own products.

It's Monday, and the Agency's inbox opened with a president describing a UFO, a pilot's haircut and a whistleblower question all in one breath. Also on the corkboard: an Olympic judoka who wants the Élysée Palace, a study on couples who split over chemtrails, and a mining company that is literally named Sasquatch. Grab the coffee. The folder is open.

XFacebookPinterest

Exhibit A: The UFO briefing, now with grooming notes

On Sunday's 60 Minutes, interviewer Ross Douthat asked President Trump whether anything he'd seen or been told made him think there's more to the UFO story. According to NewsNation and Fox News, he said he's "not too much of a believer," then recalled Air Force pilots telling him something "round" and "not aerodynamic" zipped past their F-22 at three times its speed. He also, per Metro, praised their "beautiful, nice flat tops" and said they look "like Tom Cruise."

The Agency has questions, mostly about barbers. But the real skeptic note comes from IBTimes UK: in earlier retellings the object went four times faster, and no pilots, dates, radar data or footage were offered. That isn't a scandal, it's how every story told at every Thanksgiving works. The fish gets bigger, the UFO gets faster. Believers will call this a soft-disclosure wink. Critics will call it a distraction. Both teams should notice that nobody, including the man telling the story, has shown a single piece of data yet. Asked about a secret program hidden even from presidents, he said he hadn't heard of it, but he'd "find out about it right after this interview." We'll hold for that memo.

Exhibit B: Neither left nor right, just a black belt

Across the Atlantic, five-time Olympic judo champion Teddy Riner announced he's running for president of France in 2027, calling himself neither left-wing nor right-wing and promising to "bring people together," per The Guardian and France 24. AFP reports he'd like to compete at the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics if elected, "if the position allows me to." First he needs 500 endorsements from elected officials.

"Neither left nor right" is the most-used slogan in politics, right after "I'm not a politician." But we admire the honesty of a man who could physically throw a filibuster across the room. Every pundit is calling it a long shot. Every pundit also said a lot of things last year. The Agency is filing this under "weird but legal" and buying popcorn in bulk.

Exhibit C: Breaking up over chemtrails

Here's the headline that made the rounds: "Conspiracy Theories End 1 in 5 Relationships." The actual study, from researchers at the University of Kent and the Slovak Academy of Sciences, surveyed 493 UK adults. More than 20% said they'd had a relationship end at least once because of conflicts over conspiracy beliefs, and about a third said those fights came up at least sometimes in past relationships. That's not the same as one in five relationships ending, which is a small but perfect example of how a real finding gets a dramatic glow-up on the way to your feed. The useful part: those conflicts went along with lower trust and closeness. Translation: if date night keeps turning into a lecture about birds, it might not be the birds.

Exhibit D: Odd-news side quests

Bigfoot had a busy news cycle. A driver reportedly called police after spotting a "giant soaking wet" Bigfoot (Female First), which raises the obvious question of whether cryptids own umbrellas. Meanwhile a company called Sasquatch Resources agreed to buy something called the Echo Property for $131,250 (Investing.com). It's a mining deal, not a cryptid land grab, but we can't prove Bigfoot isn't on the board. And in sports, Bill Simmons floated a theory that Golden State's Kristaps Porziņģis signing "was intentional" (Basketball Network). A basketball podcaster suspecting a plan: truly the deepest state of all.

The pattern on the corkboard

Today's files share a theme: the story is always bigger than the evidence. The UFO got faster with each telling. The breakup stat got bigger in the headline. The candidate's slogan got vaguer as it got louder. The skeptic move doesn't change: ask for the source, read past the headline and check whether the fish has grown since last time. Preferably with snacks.
