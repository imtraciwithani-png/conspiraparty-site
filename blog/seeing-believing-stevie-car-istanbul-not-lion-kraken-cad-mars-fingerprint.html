Seeing Is Believing: Stevie Wonder’s Car Challenge

Posted 11/24/2026 · ← All field reports

Full disclosure: this is commentary and jokes. Facts come from the linked reports, so click through and judge for yourself. The party kits linked here are our own products.

Tuesday’s file is about eyewitnesses. An election official says the conspiracy crowd has left the building. A music legend invites doubters to test their theory at 60 miles an hour. A whole city hunted a lion that turned out to be not a lion. Bitcoin briefly cost $17,000, but only if you paid in loonies. And Mars left a fingerprint at the scene. Grab the red string.

XFacebookPinterest

Exhibit A: “Find a new grift”

Per ABC15, Arizona certified its 2026 results Monday, and Secretary of State Adrian Fontes said the lack of post-election protests shows the public has rejected election conspiracies. People who push them, he said, would need to “find a new grift.” The Arizona Mirror notes the canvass went off without issue, and that turnout was 59.6%, the lowest in a decade.

Fair play to everyone, which means unfair to everyone. A quiet certification day is genuinely good news, and we’ll take it. But notice the ancient law of politics: the side that wins always discovers the system works beautifully, and the side that loses always discovers it was rigged. Then they swap scripts the next cycle like a community theater rotating roles. Also, declaring conspiracy theories “done” is a bold move. That’s like announcing the end of mosquitoes because it’s November. They’re not gone, they’re just in the group chat, rebranding.

Exhibit B: The ride-along challenge

Billboard and NME report that at his Nov. 21 show in Chicago, Stevie Wonder addressed the decades-old rumor that he can secretly see. His offer: “If you believe I can really drive or really see, get in the car with me and we’ll see what happens to us at the end of the day.”

This is the single greatest debunking method ever devised, and every fact-checker should study it. No spreadsheets. No 40-tweet thread. Just: you’re so sure, buckle up. Imagine if every theory came with a ride-along. “The moon landing was faked?” Great, here’s a rocket. “Birds aren’t real?” Hold this pigeon. Wonder has been in on the joke for years, and honestly, a guy who pranked Lionel Richie by pretending to back down his own driveway has earned a lifetime membership in our skeptics’ club.

Exhibit C: The lion that wasn’t

According to AP and Goal.com, grainy security footage in Istanbul’s Sarıyer district set off a 24-hour search for a lioness, with thermal drones, bait and camera traps, right before Galatasaray hosted Aston Villa. Then the governor’s office reviewed new angles and said the animal was not a lion or any other big cat. They haven’t said what it was.

Every cryptid story in history, in one weekend. Blurry footage, mass panic, official search, official “never mind,” and a lingering mystery about what was actually in the bushes. A Galatasaray defender reportedly joked that the only lion he keeps at home is his son. Our field office’s working theory is a very confident golden retriever, and we will not be taking questions.

Exhibit D: Bitcoin’s Canadian clearance sale

CoinDesk reports that Bitcoin briefly traded for C$24,250, about $17,000 U.S., on Kraken’s Canadian dollar market early Saturday while the rest of the world priced it near $57,700. Kraken says it found no breach and the trades stand. It hasn’t named a cause, though one industry exec suggested a market maker simply ran out of money.

Crypto fans spent years warning that the big crash would be engineered by shadowy elites. Instead it was apparently one order book in Canada running out of change, like a vending machine at 3 a.m. Somewhere a Canadian is holding cheap Bitcoin and apologizing to it.

Bonus exhibit: Mars leaves a fingerprint

ScienceAlert covers HiRISE images of Daedalia Planum, where boulders sit in long curving rows that look like a giant fingerprint. Scientists aren’t sure why. Freeze-thaw cycles or quake shaking (the “Brazil nut effect”) are on the table. Aliens are not. Sorry. We checked. It’s rocks being organized, which is more than we can say for our junk drawer.

The pattern on the corkboard

Today’s theme is “seeing is believing,” and the problem is that seeing isn’t always seeing. A grainy video made a lion. A thin order book made a crash. A quiet day made a victory lap. A row of rocks made a fingerprint. Before you believe your eyes, check what’s outside the frame. And if anybody offers you a ride to prove their point, maybe take the bus.
