Faxes, Footnotes and a Very Old Doorstop

Posted 11/13/2026 · ← All field reports

Full disclosure: this is commentary and jokes. Facts come from the linked reports, so click through and judge for yourself. The party kits linked here are our own products.

It's Friday the 13th, and the Agency's corkboard is covered in old technology and fresh clarifications. Global finance rediscovered the fax machine. NATO's boss explained who he meant. A podcast guest says the president isn't cleared for the good UFO files. And a Czech barn spent years standing on a priceless Bronze Age artifact. Grab your red string. Also your dial tone.

XFacebookPinterest

Exhibit A: The fax machine's revenge tour

A worldwide Microsoft 365 outage knocked out Outlook, Teams and Copilot for millions of people, and traders went back to phones, WhatsApp and fax machines, per Bloomberg. In Japan, market participants used fax to confirm trades after email stopped working, reports The Japan Times. Reuters says firms in Taiwan confirmed some trades by fax too, Euronext told listed companies to "pick up the phone," and the insurer Aviva moved earnings-call recordings around by Bluetooth, per Investing.com. Microsoft blamed server problems and said it wasn't aware of any malicious activity.

The Agency would like to formally apologize to the fax machine. For twenty years we called it obsolete. We used it as a punchline. And the second the cloud sneezed, the whole planet ran back to the beige box in the corner like it was an ex who still had a key. Somewhere in a supply closet, a fax machine is printing a single page that just says "told you." Every conspiracy theorist who refused to give up their landline is having the best day of their lives.

Exhibit B: The footnote heard round Europe

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte told Dutch TV that allies in Spain and Portugal sometimes say the war in Ukraine "is far away," and Portugal's foreign ministry called that regrettable, per Notícias ao Minuto (Lusa). Arriving at a summit in Dublin, Rutte said he has "no criticism of Portugal or Spain" and was talking about "the populations - not the politicians, but the people in the country," according to NATO's own transcript. Asked if he'd meet Portugal's prime minister to explain, he said: "I don't think I have anything to explain."

The Agency admires this move, because it is the purest form of political spin ever recorded. Step one: say a thing. Step two: clarify that you weren't insulting the government, you were insulting... the people. Step three: announce there's nothing to explain, immediately after explaining. That's not a walk-back, that's a moonwalk. Meanwhile Lisbon's foreign ministry got to be very offended on X, which, to be fair, is also a NATO-approved national sport.

Exhibit C: The president, apparently, needs a hall pass

On the November 6 Tucker Carlson Show, former Force Recon Marine Chad Robichaux said, "I don't think President Trump has access to these programs right now," meaning secret UFO programs, and pointed to Majestic-12, per a transcript of the episode. When asked whether some programs sit outside presidential authority, Carlson said, "I know they are. I've talked to Trump about it," but didn't say what Trump told him, notes UAP Trail, which also points out that the MJ-12 documents are disputed and the "real footage" of an orb mentioned on the show wasn't shown.

Let's run the logic. The most powerful man on Earth can launch a missile, pardon a turkey and post at 3 a.m., but he can't get into the alien filing cabinet. Who has the key? A guy named Gary in a basement with a lanyard? The Agency is skeptical, mostly because no government that can't keep its email running for twelve hours is hiding a flawless, 80-year, multi-president alien secret. They'd have to fax the cover-up memo. And we've seen the fax line today. It's busy.

Exhibit D: The world's most overqualified doorstop

And now a palate cleanser. A homeowner in Morkůvky, Czech Republic, noticed a rectangular stone poking out of his garden in 2007. It had helped hold up the foundation of an old barn. It turned out to be half of a roughly 3,000-year-old mold for casting bronze spearheads, per ScienceAlert. A Moravian Museum archaeologist called it "the best preserved and most perfect casting mold for a bronze spearhead in Central Europe," and it was likely imported from northern Hungary, reports Live Science. The owner donated it to the museum in 2019.

This is the most relatable story of the week. Every one of us has a priceless relic quietly doing a terrible job somewhere in our house. Grandma's crystal holding open the screen door. A first-edition book under a wobbly table leg. The Agency is now checking every brick in the office. If one of them turns out to be a Bronze Age ray gun mold, we will tell absolutely everyone, then fax it to the president, who probably isn't cleared to see it.

The pattern on the corkboard

Today's theme: the old stuff keeps winning. The fax machine saved global finance. A diplomat used the oldest trick in politics, the clarification. A podcast dusted off a 1940s theory. And a 3,000-year-old mold out-performed a modern barn foundation. The skeptic move is the boring one: read the transcript, check the source, and keep a working landline. Then laugh.
